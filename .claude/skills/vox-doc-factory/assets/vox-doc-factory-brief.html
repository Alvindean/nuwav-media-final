2The SEAL Team

A capability gap is a mission, not a blocker. Five roles, one loop.

The Commander writes a testable definition of done before anyone searches anything. Then Recon finds the sources, Intel extracts the real technique, Breacher builds the thing, and Proof tries to break it. The Commander is the only one who can call it done.

THE SOURCE HIERARCHY — THIS IS THE IMPORTANT PART

Tier 1 — ask the live system. APIs, MCP tools, schema dumps. Almost always skipped, almost always best.

Tier 2 — official docs and source code. Code is truth; docs are intent.
Tier 3 — GitHub issues, Stack Overflow, the subreddits where practitioners are.
Tier 4 — YouTube transcripts, pulled with yt-dlp, not watched.
Tier 5 — blogs. Last, because content farms rewrite each other, so three agreeing sources may be one source.

Higher tier always wins a disagreement, and anything below Tier 2 gets labelled as an estimate.

YouTube's upload module has a required field called containsSyntheticMedia. For a channel with AI-generated visuals, that has to be true.

MISSION 2 — CAPCUT MASTERY · WRITTEN DOWN

Two doctrines, because they're genuinely different jobs and mixing them ruins both.

VIRAL — cuts every 0.8–2 seconds, beat-synced to the waveform, built to survive the first 3 seconds.
CINEMATIC — cuts every 3.5–4.5 seconds, driven by the narration, built to survive minute 8.

Exact settings, not principles — which easing curve per move type, the two speed-curve shapes, when optical flow beats frame blending and when it smears, the parallax layer ratios, the J-cut and L-cut frame counts, blend modes and opacity ranges.

THE NUMBERS IT'S ALL AIMED AT
First 10–15 seconds — hook is working>70%
First 10–15 seconds — exceptional>80%
First 10–15 seconds — hook is broken<50%
10–20 min video, overall retention40–55%
Retention editing lift (J/L cuts, 20–30s changes)+20–30% AVD

The first 30 seconds now works as a standalone ranking input — weighted separately from the rest of the video — and the first 3 seconds decide whether distribution opens up or gets throttled. That's why both doctrines spend so much effort there.

1Why this is affordable

A 15-minute video is 900 seconds. Generating 900 seconds of AI video would cost thousands of credits an episode.

But that isn't what Vox actually is. Vox is photos, maps and charts with camera moves applied in the edit. Real generated motion is the exception.

So the factory splits screen time three ways — and that split is the whole reason 2 to 3 episodes a week works.

Stills with a camera move70% · ~0.4 credits/sec
Motion graphics — maps, charts, type18% · free
Hero generated motion12% · ~2 credits/sec
THE THREE RULES THAT KEEP CREDITS DOWN

1. Turn the model's audio off. Every clip gets sound: "off". We never use generated audio — ElevenLabs carries the whole track. Leaving it on is a surcharge on every single clip.

2. Every moving shot starts from a still we already made. Image-to-video costs less than text-to-video, and it guarantees the clip matches everything around it.

3. Generate at final size. Stills come out at 2K or 4K, so they never need upscaling. Upscaling costs more than the resolution did.

5The nine stages

Each stage ends at a gate. A failed gate goes back one stage — it never goes forward "with a note".

STAGE 1Intake & Lock — the topic comes in from your profitability system. Out comes a one-sentence thesis and the single question the episode answers.
Gate: if the thesis needs two sentences, it's two videos. Split it.
STAGE 2Research & Fact Pack — dated timeline, key numbers with sources, primary documents, two counter-arguments.
Fact Gate: every on-screen claim has a named source with a URL and a date. Unsourced numbers get cut.
STAGE 3Script — 2,150 to 2,450 words in the five-act shape, every paragraph carrying a bracketed visual note.
Read-Aloud Gate: read it out loud and time it. Under 11 minutes, it goes back.
STAGE 4Narration — the VO is made before any visuals. It's the spine; picture gets cut to it.
Runtime Gate: the audio file has to be 11:00 or longer. Checked here, not at the end.
STAGE 5Style Key — palette, illustration treatment, typography, how archival is handled, six reference frames.
Style Lock: nothing generates before this. It's what makes 200 frames look like one episode.
STAGE 6Shot Design — the shot list, built against the narration's word-level timestamps.
Boredom Audit: any 15-second window with no new information gets a shot added.
STAGE 7Generation — the Higgsfield run, with the credit ledger.
Budget Gate: over the ceiling, hero clips get downgraded to stills before generating.
STAGE 8Edit, Grade, Finish — assembly, the transition playbook, the house grade, loudness.
Retention Gate: watch it once unpaused. Every second you wanted to skip gets fixed.
STAGE 9Package & Publish — title, thumbnail, description with sources, chapters, upload.
Human Publish: it lands private or scheduled. You flip it public.

6Which models, and why

THE STILLS ENGINE — 70% OF THE EPISODE

Nano Banana Pro at 2K for anything with text on it — chart labels, map legends, quote cards, title cards. It's the best text renderer in the catalogue, and Vox style is dense with on-screen labels. A model that garbles typography costs more in re-tries than it saves.

Seedream 4.5 for wide establishing plates. Renders to 4K on the basic tier.

Nano Banana 2 at 1K for background textures and paper grounds.

THE MOTION ENGINE — 12 TO 18 CLIPS AN EPISODE

Kling 3.0 Turbo at 720p, 5 seconds, started from a still we already made. This is the working hero shot — 10 to 14 of them per episode.

Kling 3.0 in standard mode with sound off for the 3 or 4 signature shots — act openers and the big reveal.

Seedance 2.0 Mini when a subject has to stay identical across several shots.

THE ONE TO AVOID

Veo 3.1 costs 40 to 70 credits a clip — that's 15 to 35 stills for one shot.

If the cold open genuinely needs it, generate it in Google Flow instead. You already pay for that, so at the margin it's free, and it takes the single most expensive line item out of the Higgsfield budget completely.

UPSCALING — SELECTIVE, NOT ROUTINE

Stills never get upscaled. They're already generated above timeline resolution.

A 720p hero clip that lands full-frame goes through Bytedance Video Upscale to 1080p with the aigc preset — that preset is tuned for generated footage and handles the soft areas AI video produces far better than the default.

A clip inside a split-screen or behind type gets nothing. It's displayed at under half size — the upscale is invisible. Budget about 6 upscales an episode, not 15.

9The transitions — and the CapCut problem

The difference between "AI slideshow" and "Vox documentary" is almost entirely how shots connect, not how they look.

The rule underneath all of it: a transition is a camera decision, not an effect. Nothing in this workflow uses CapCut's built-in transition presets. Every move is built from position, scale, blur and speed keyframes on the clips themselves.

THE TWELVE MOVES

1. Axial cut-in — same angle, tighter. Snaps attention onto a number.
2. The slow push — 100% to 106% across the shot. This is 70% of the episode.
3. Whip-pan match — blur across the seam. Changes era without a title card.
4. Shape match cut — a round thing cuts to a round thing. The moment people remember.
5. Speed-ramp punch — compresses "and then twelve years passed".
6. Parallax layer slide — three layers at three speeds. The paper-cutout look.
7. Track-back with blur — the Vox signature. Pull from a detail to the whole, 4px of blur at the fastest point.
8. Map hand-off — an Earth Studio zoom ends on a still with a matched horizon.
9. J-cut — narration starts 12 frames before picture. Roughly every third cut.
10. L-cut — sound holds into the next shot. Every act change.
11. Graphic wipe — a growing chart bar covers frame and reveals the next shot.
12. Cut on motion — the eye is already travelling, so it never sees the cut.

Every one has its exact CapCut keyframe values written down in the repo, plus the placement rules — cut on the stressed syllable, never two identical moves in a row, and when the narrator pauses for effect the picture stops moving too.

THE HONEST PROBLEM

Everything else in this pipeline runs unattended. Stage 8 does not.

A 15-minute timeline is about 200 shots, each needing keyframed moves and cut timing against the narration. CapCut has no API and no scripting — so a person builds that timeline. Realistically 2 to 4 hours an episode once the templates exist. That's 6 to 8 hours a week at two episodes.

Three ways to close that gap

OPTION A — HUMAN EDITOR

$150–300 an episode, or your own 2–4 hours. Best quality — CapCut's real strength, the transition grammar, gets used properly.

OPTION B — PROGRAMMATIC ASSEMBLY

FFmpeg builds the whole timeline automatically from the shot list and the narration timings. Your capcut-pro-editor skill already has an auto mode doing exactly this. Genuinely hands-off — but the transitions are cruder than a human's.

OPTION C — HYBRID · RECOMMENDED

Programmatic assembly builds the full rough cut — pushes, cuts landed on the narration's word timings, grade and captions applied. Then a human spends 45 minutes upgrading the 15 to 20 transitions that actually carry the episode.

You keep the thing that makes it look like Vox and you lose the three hours of mechanical timeline work. And Option B stays as the fallback — if a week gets busy, it still ships a publishable episode with nobody touching it.
